Appendix · structural framing

Ideas when deploying

Two structural ideas to have in mind when the system is actually rolled out: how it grows in maturity, and how it thinks about mitigating import risk. Kept separate from the plan so the plan itself stays focused on the engine and the data.

Deployment idea 1

Two parts, three stages of maturity

The ten stages split into two layers, and the whole system grows in maturity over time. Get the foundation right first — the forecast layer and the shift to real-time both depend on it.

Part 1 · Stages 01–05

Foundation — the definitive variables

The fixed set of numbers that describe what we need, who can supply it, and what it truly costs delivered. These variables don’t forecast anything — they define reality as it stands today.

Part 2 · Stages 06–10

Forecast — time and influence added on top

Everything in Part 2 is layered on the numbers Part 1 gives us. It takes the definitive variables and adds time plus anything that could change those variables — scrap, energy, FX, PMIs, quota burn — and turns that into a forward view and a decision.

Three stages of maturity

How the system grows from a review cadence into real-time decisions

The two-part structure holds at every level of maturity. What changes is the cadence at which the numbers flow in and the trigger for a decision.

  1. 01

    Now

    Refine the current process

    Formalise what today is done ad-hoc: monthly / quarterly reviews of stock, consumption, supplier quotes and landed cost. Standardise the equations, source every variable from a named field, retire spreadsheets that don’t map to Part 1’s schemas. Delivers a much better version of what already exists.

  2. 02

    Next

    Move to a live data feed

    Instead of pulling numbers into the equations on review day, the Part 1 databases update continuously — HMRC quotas, MEPS, FX, supplier quotes, stock. The equations resolve on demand. The forecast layer (Part 2) now has fresh inputs whenever it’s asked to run.

  3. 03

    End state

    Real-time flagged decision-making

    The system watches its own outputs. When a variable moves past a defined threshold — quota burn accelerates, FX shifts, a supplier quote lands outside benchmark, stock cover drops — it flags a decision automatically, with the exemplar sentence already drafted. Humans approve or override. No more waiting for the next review.

Deployment idea 2

Mitigating import risk — DDP is one option among several

The real goal isn’t to buy DDP. It’s to protect the margin under uncertainty. Every import quote carries hidden variance — quota might exhaust mid-shipment, tariff might change, freight might spike, FX might move — and if we buy on headline price alone and one of those hits, the margin evaporates. DDP is one way to shift that risk off our balance sheet. There are others. The engine’s job is to compare them on a risk-adjusted, expected-value basis and find the arbitrage.

The lens

Expected value, not headline price

Every buying strategy has an expected cost per tonne, computed as a probability-weighted sum across scenarios: what we pay if quota holds, if it exhausts, if FX moves, if freight spikes. That number — not the quote — is what we compare.

E[cost] = Σ P(scenario) × cost(scenario)

Where the arbitrage is

When a protected strategy costs less than an exposed one

Sometimes the “safer” option (DDP, domestic, front-loaded) has a lower expected cost than the cheapest headline non-DDP price once quota, duty and FX risk are priced in. That gap is the arbitrage. Sometimes it goes the other way — and the engine says take the exposure and pocket the difference.

The mitigation options the engine compares side by side

Strategy Who carries the risk Best when Hidden cost / trade-off
DDP purchase Supplier / trader Tariff or quota exposure high; supplier better placed to hedge or absorb DDP price often includes a tariff-cap premium — buyer effectively pays for the insurance
Non-DDP + hedge Buyer, offset by FX / freight instruments FX and freight are the dominant risks; quota is safe Hedge premium eats a slice of margin; adds operational complexity
Domestic (Tata Steel UK) No import risk at all Quota exhausted or exhausting; short lead time critical Higher unit price; 30-day payment terms strain working capital
Front-load (buy earlier) Buyer, but ahead of the risk event Quota burn accelerating; MEPS trending up; supplier prices soft today Extra working capital tied up in stock; storage; obsolescence risk
Origin swap New supplier chain Original origin’s quota nearly exhausted; alternative origin has headroom Onboarding time; supplier discovery cost; quality re-qualification
Mixed sourcing Split between routes Concentration risk unacceptable; want optionality across quotas / suppliers More paperwork; smaller volumes lose per-tonne leverage
What we capture on every quote to make this comparison possible
  • Supplier / trader · underlying mill (where known)
  • Product · origin · commodity code
  • Price £/t · Incoterm · DDP y/n
  • Transport included · duty included · tariff-cap arrangement
  • Quota category · expected quota status at shipment date
  • Lead time · MOQ · validity date · payment terms

These fields feed the expected-value calculation directly — without them, DDP vs non-DDP is a headline-price comparison instead of a risk-adjusted one.
